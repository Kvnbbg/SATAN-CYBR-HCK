# The Modern Cybercrime Economy: Old Weaknesses, New Attack Surfaces

The modern cybercrime economy works like this: attackers do not need to invent a new technique every time. They profit by combining new attack surfaces with old, unpatched weaknesses, then monetizing access through theft, fraud, and extortion. A few patterns stand out: Longstanding vulnerabilities remain lucrative. Unpatched VPNs, exposed RDP, outdated web frameworks, misconfigured cloud storage, and weak credential hygiene still provide initial access. Attackers scan at scale and exploit whatever is left exposed. New technology creates new trust gaps. Cloud identity, APIs, cross-chain bridges, DNS/BGP routing, and telecom systems become high-value targets because compromising them can redirect trust rather than break cryptography. Ransomware has evolved into data extortion. Many crews now steal data first and threaten publication or regulatory exposure even if backups make encryption less effective. This is “double extortion,” and sometimes triple extortion with DDoS or customer pressure. Cryptocurrency theft and ransomware overlap. State-linked groups and criminal affiliates use phishing, SIM swapping, infrastructure compromise, and malware to steal credentials and keys, then launder proceeds through mixers and exchanges. The weakest link is often the supply chain or a trusted intermediary. A compromised developer, MSP, DNS registrar, cloud provider, or bridge verifier can give attackers far more leverage than a direct frontal attack. The defensive implication is that patch management alone is not enough, and advanced technology alone is not enough. Organizations need layered controls: phishing-resistant MFA, least privilege, network segmentation, immutable backups, DNS/BGP security, cloud configuration monitoring, supply-chain review, and tested incident response. The attackers are skilled, but they are also opportunistic—most incidents still start with a known weakness that was not fixed, monitored, or contained.
